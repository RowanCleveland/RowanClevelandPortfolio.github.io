Bridge Design Research

An experimental comparative analysis quantifying bridge span as a determinant of carrying capacity in truss designs.

Research Question

My research investigated the relationship between bridge span and carrying capacity across three different truss bridge designs. Bridge span is the parallel distance between two supporting structures holding the weight of the bridge, while carrying capacity is the maximum amount of weight that the bridge can hold at a time before failure.

I focused on Baltimore, Pennsylvania, and K-truss designs and compared their performance using regression models. These specific truss designs were selected because they are some of the most relevant and prevalent truss bridge designs in modern society. The designs also represent many of the base designs that other trusses reference. My research investigated how the relationship between span and carrying capacity could be comparatively quantified for these three bridge designs.

Experimental Design

I created physical bridge models using PLA filament and tested their ability to carry increasing loads. I designed the bridges in Onshape, an online CAD (Computer Aided Design) program, which allowed precise measurements and reduced the risk of variability between models. I created models of each bridge design at three different controlled lengths: 6 inches, 12 inches, and 18 inches. Each bridge maintained the same height of three inches, and I placed each vertical member the same distance apart. With advice from civil engineer Tim Wiess, who aided in the formation of this method, I created each model to limit the only primary variable to span length.

Data Collection

I used the P1S Bambu Labs 3D printer to create the bridges with PLA filament. I chose 3D printing because it creates bridges that are nearly identical to the design created online and reduces fluctuations between the bridges. I fastened each bridge to the same platforms to limit variables that could unexpectedly skew the data. I attached a reinforced bucket to thin metal pieces arranged across the center of the bridge, distributing the weight evenly in the center third of the bridge span. I added weights in standard increments of 1 pound every 5 seconds until the bridge collapsed or gave out.

I controlled bridge height, materials, method of construction, load placement, and load increment. The independent variables were bridge span size and bridge design, and the dependent variable was the maximum center load that could be applied. I stabilized each weight on the bridge for at least 5 seconds before determining the bridge's condition. To reduce external factors that may unrealistically skew the data, I conducted tests indoors on a flat surface, leveled the bridges, and recorded the process with a camera set up level to the bridge. For this study, catastrophic failure meant that the bridge broke in four or more members.

Statistical Analysis

I analyzed the difference between the carrying capacity of the two trials for each design and length to determine repeatability and calculate the standard deviation and percent deviation. These deviation measures allowed me to visualize how different each test was from another for each length, with a lower deviation indicating that the results were likely more consistent and reliable. I used both standard deviation in pounds and percent deviation because, for stronger bridges, the numerical deviation may be high even if the bridges were relatively close in maximum weight compared to weaker bridges.

I plotted the data on graphs depicting maximum weight versus span length and applied multiple common regression models, including linear, exponential, power, logarithmic, and polynomial. I analyzed each regression model based on its coefficient of determination, or R-squared value, which quantifies how well the model fits the given data. A value of over 0.85 typically indicates a strong correlation between the data and the regression model. I compared the average R-squared values for each model to determine which regression type most closely aligned with the data and standard conventions. The polynomial model had the highest average R-squared value at 0.973, but because it could easily overfit the data with only three different span lengths, I concluded that the power model was the best overall regression model, with an average R-squared value of 0.958.

Research Paper

The project resulted in a full AP Research paper examining the experimental results and their implications for bridge design. The power model produced numerical equations relating span to maximum weight for each bridge design: Pennsylvania: y = 6827.3x^-1.423, Baltimore: y = 2939.4x^-1.038, and K-truss: y = 891.19x^-.523. In these models, y represents the maximum weight carried at that length and x represents the span length in inches of the bridge. The coefficient represents the predicted carrying capacity of the bridge if the span length was one inch, and the exponent indicates the rate of decline in strength as span increases.

My findings demonstrated that the relationship between span and carrying capacity was inverse and nonlinear. Both the Pennsylvania and Baltimore truss bridges had relatively high carrying capacities at 6 inches, while the K-truss held the most weight at 18 inches. The K-truss data also had more irregularities because several bridges broke at the center where the weight was applied rather than in multiple joints. This suggested that the specific segments where the weight was applied became more of a limiting factor than the span or the truss design itself. This project gave me experience designing an experiment, collecting physical data, analyzing results, and communicating technical findings.
